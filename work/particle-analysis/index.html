Dynamic analysis of microscopic particles

Measuring size, shape, and anomalies in particles moving along a conveyor at 60 frames per second — the research that produced two publications and taught me that the model is rarely the bottleneck.

RV College of EngineeringResearcher, RV College of Engineering · 2023 — 2024

  • Computer vision
  • Image analysis
  • Research
  • Publications

The problem

Particle characterisation is conventionally done with laser diffraction and similar instrumentation. We asked whether optical imaging could recover size, shape, and anomaly information from particles in motion, captured at 60 frames per second as they travelled along a conveyor.

The appeal of imaging is that it measures shape directly rather than inferring it from a scattering model. The difficulty is everything else.

Why it was hard

Classroom computer vision gives you clean, static, well-lit inputs. This gave us none of those:

  • Lighting was never uniform across the field of view, and it drifted over a session.
  • Motion blur scaled with particle velocity, so the same particle imaged twice was not the same measurement twice.
  • Orientation was uncontrolled. A flat particle seen edge-on and face-on produces wildly different projections, and nothing in a single frame tells you which you got.
  • Particles touched and overlapped, and segmenting a cluster into constituents is a guess dressed up as a measurement.
  • Ground truth was scarce, which is the real problem — without it, you cannot tell a genuine anomaly from a processing artefact.

Approach

Most of the work went into the parts that are not the model: controlling acquisition so the inputs were comparable, normalising for illumination before anything downstream ran, and treating a particle’s trajectory across frames as the measurement unit rather than any single frame. Tracking a particle through several frames gives several views of it, which does more for orientation ambiguity than any amount of per-frame cleverness.

Evaluation had to be built as deliberately as the pipeline. Where ground truth was thin, we leaned on consistency checks — does the same particle measured across its trajectory agree with itself — as a proxy for correctness.

Outcome

Two peer-reviewed publications:

  1. Micro-Particle Anomaly Detection & Shape Classification — IEEE Conference, Vellore. The foundational work on identifying anomalies and classifying shape.
  2. Dynamic Image Analysis of Microscopic Particles — Springer CCIS, MIND Conference. The follow-up extending the approach to particles in motion.

What I took from it

This project is the reason I think about deployment conditions before architecture. The effectiveness of a machine learning system cannot be assessed through the performance of its model alone — data acquisition, hardware limits, throughput, and the conditions it will actually run under decide whether it works in practice. We could improve a classifier for a week and get less than we got from fixing the illumination.

It was also my first experience of a problem with no predetermined solution, where the work was deciding what the question was.
